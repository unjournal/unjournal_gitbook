Current focus, October 2026

Rapid expert evaluation of AI economics and governance research

Research on the economic effects of AI, the pace of AI progress, and AI governance is shaping real decisions, and most of it has had no independent expert review. The Unjournal is commissioning public, quantified evaluations of the most decision-relevant work. We aim to provide expert discussion, critique, and signals of credibility and usefulness in weeks rather than yearsAt top economics journals, the average time from submission to acceptance was just over two years for articles published in 2012–13, against about six months at leading science journals (Hadavand, Hamermesh & Wilson, JEL 2024). That counts only the journal that finally accepts a paper. Most papers circulate as working papers first and are rejected elsewhere before that, so the full path is usually longer. Ellison (2002) documents how this lag grew..

Why this, and why now

How fast will AI capabilities grow and replace human work? How will gains flow to labor versus capital? Will revenue and compute concentrate in a few firms and countries? Will regulation change which models are released where? These questions bear on AI-safety strategy, philanthropic funding, regulation, public spending, and international controls.

Economists, forecasters, and policy analysts are moving into this area fast. Growth theorists are co-authoring scenario models with frontier labs. Forecasting researchers are eliciting beliefs from economists, AI-company staff, and superforecasters. Policy organizations are producing quantitative estimates of compute smuggling and regulatory delays. Some of this work is already being used: in fiscal scenarios, in commentary on monetary policy, and in legislative fights.

Little of it has had careful, public, expert scrutiny. Academic journal review would provide that, but it takes years and reveals only which journal accepted the paper. By then much of this work is out of date. Informal critique in blogs and threads is faster, but it is self-selected and rarely carries legible expertise. We aim to fill the gap: neutral, paid, expert evaluation, published quickly, with ratings and claim-level assessment.

How it works

We are neither a journal nor a forum thread. Evaluators write a referee-style report with quantified ratings and uncertainty, and we publish it alongside the authors' response and a management synthesis.

1. Prioritize

We screen for decision relevance, apparent influence, checkable claims, and timing, with input from an advisory group and public ratings.

2. Commission

We match each paper with balanced expertise from our pool of 200+ vetted evaluators and beyond, check conflicts, and pay for careful critique with incentives for promptness.

3. Publish

Evaluations, ratings, and author responses appear publicly with DOIs, usually within about a month of commissioning for this stream.

4. Synthesize

We summarize which claims hold up, which assumptions drive the results, and what a decision-maker should and should not rely on.

We ask whether research is credible and useful, not whether it is novel or "publishable." We have no stake in which way a result comes out: we do not produce AI research, hold a policy agenda, or take funding from AI labs.

Where we are

We have briefs on 34 candidate papers, generated with AI and each given some human review: what each claims, why it matters, how checkable it is, and how much scrutiny it has already had. Two are prioritized for evaluation now; more follow as evaluators and funding allow.

  • Forecasting the Economic Effects of AI Karger et al., Forecasting Research Institute. Structured forecasts from economists, AI-company staff, superforecasters, and the public on growth, labor-force participation, and more. Already used in fiscal scenarios and policy commentary. Evaluation focus: sampling, question design, aggregation, and economic interpretation.
  • Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI Korinek et al., Anthropic Institute. Maps AI-capability paths onto GDP, labor share, wages, and unemployment to 2030. A likely anchor for the redistribution debate. Evaluation focus: model structure, sensitivity to assumptions, calibration, and scenario selection.
  • Potentially next Candidates under consideration include the economics of recursive self-improvement; estimates of compute smuggling to China; game-theoretic models of AI race dynamics; AI liability and insurance.

Scope: AI governance and regulation, the rate of AI progress, and AI's effects on growth, labor, and the concentration of economic and political power. Not technical AI safety or interpretability. The briefs are AI-generated, checked against sources, and reviewed by at least one person on our team; corrections are welcome.

How to help

  1. Tell us which claims you rely on. If you or your organization use this research in decisions, funding, or agenda-setting, we want to know which claims matter and what would change if they were wrong.
  2. Suggest evaluators. We need people in expert elicitation and survey methods, macroeconomics and growth theory applied to AI, labor economics, and the technical side of AI. Send suggestions or self-nominations privately to contact@unjournal.org, or join the evaluator pool.
  3. Rate and nominate papers. Rate and discuss the shortlist, or submit research we have missed. A low priority score is not a judgment of quality.
  4. Fund or partner. Evaluator compensation and speed depend on funding. If you want independent evaluation of research your organization produces or relies on, write to contact@unjournal.org.
